What the data says

Full dataset, no filters. Every number here can be reproduced from the other pages with filters cleared.

1

Late delivery is the single biggest swing in ratings

Late orders average 2.27★ against 4.29★ on time, a 2.02-point gap. It is not a state or seller mix effect: the gap is 2.005 points within customer states (21 states) and 1.971 within sellers (287 sellers). More than half of late orders (53.8%) get a 1★ review, against 6.6% on time.

2

The damage starts on day one and is mostly done by day seven

Average review is 4.03 on the promised date, 3.73 at one day late and 1.91 at seven days late, then flattens. Even arriving on the last allowed day costs points: orders arriving 15+ days early average 4.32.

3

Carrier transit is where late orders lose time

Late orders spend 27.8 days in transit on average against 8.0 for on-time orders, while approval barely differs (0.5 vs 0.4 days). Seller handling also doubles (5.6 vs 2.7 days), so it contributes, but transit is the larger share.

4

Missed seller handoffs nearly quadruple the late rate

9.0% of orders were picked up after the seller's shipping limit. Those orders were late 20.8% of the time against 5.4% otherwise. Still, only 27.8% of late orders had a missed handoff, so most lateness happens after the parcel leaves the seller.

5

Distance and the Northeast

Late rate climbs from 4.5% under 100 km to 11.6% beyond 1,500 km; same-state orders are late 4.5% of the time against 8.0% interstate. About 71% of orders ship from São Paulo sellers, so Northeast customers sit at the end of the longest routes: 12.7% late with 20 days average delivery, against 4.5% and 8.8 days for São Paulo customers. Alagoas (21.4%) and Maranhão (17.4%) are the worst states with 100+ orders; Rio de Janeiro is the biggest by volume of late orders.

6

Promises do not absorb route variability

Northeast promises are already longer (31 days vs 20 for São Paulo), yet still missed more often. Rio de Janeiro has a similar average buffer to São Paulo (11.7 vs 11.0 days) but nearly three times the late rate, so it is the unpredictability of a route, not the average, that breaks promises. Across states, smaller buffers go with higher late rates (Spearman ρ = −0.70 across all 27 states).

7

Peaks break the network

November 2017 (Black Friday) hit 12.4% late; February and March 2018 hit 14.1% and 19.0%, with median transit rising from about 7 to 10–11 days. Promises were not lengthened for March (22.7 days on average, below December's 28.3). By August 2018, promises had shrunk to 15.8 days while late rate rose again; recent months are also affected by orders still undelivered at data cutoff, so treat them carefully.

8

Late customers speak up, and may not return

58.9% of reviewed late orders include a written comment against 39.3% on time, so lateness creates visible public complaints. Among first-time customers, 2.5% came back after a late first order against 3.0% after an on-time one. Repeat buying is rare on Olist overall, so treat this as a signal rather than proof.

9

Product mix matters less than route

Among categories with 500+ orders, late rate ranges only from 4.2% (luggage) to 8.1% (baby, office furniture), a narrower spread than distance. Multi-item orders are late less often (5.4% for two items vs 6.9% for one) yet score lower (3.69 vs 4.21), which points to a separate problem such as split shipments.

Recommended actions

  1. Set promises by route, not by average. Use origin–destination distance and recent transit variance to set the promised date, with extra buffer on long Northeast lanes and peak months.
  2. Work the carrier lanes first. Transit time is the main gap; audit carrier performance on SP→Northeast and SP→RJ routes, where volume and delay meet.
  3. Enforce the seller handoff. Alert sellers before the shipping limit and track missed handoffs as a seller KPI; prioritise sellers in the watchlist with many late orders.
  4. Warn before the promise breaks. Proactive messages when an order is predicted to be late could soften the 1★ cliff; test this with a controlled pilot.
  5. Plan for peaks. Lengthen promises and add carrier capacity before Black Friday and the February–March period.

Scenario: cutting the late rate from 6.77% to 4.00% means about 2,675 fewer late orders and an illustrative average review lift of about 0.055 points.

Limits of this analysis

  • Observational data: carrier, product, price and promise-setting can affect both delivery and review. Gaps are associations, not causal effects.
  • 2,963 orders not marked delivered and 8 delivered orders without dates are excluded, so the worst failures may be missing.
  • Distance uses zip-prefix centroids and the highest-value item's seller; it is approximate.
  • Review score is ordinal; the formal test is a two-sided Mann–Whitney U (p < 1e-300).
  • Historical Brazil data from 2016–2018 may not describe today's operations.

Late means the delivery calendar date is after the promised date. Late rate uses all delivered orders; review averages use reviewed orders only (latest answered review per order). Seller filters and category use the seller and category of each order's highest-value item; the seller watchlist counts each order once per participating seller.

Data: Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist (Kaggle), anonymized public data.
